Frankendael is the only remaining country estate in the Watergraafsmeer. Dozens of estates once lined the Middenweg. During the Dutch Golden Age, wealthy Amsterdammers developed areas well beyond the city where country estates were founded: far from the bustle, an owner could withdraw with close company to enjoy nature, the harvest of fruit and vegetables, and the quiet of country life. The historic Frankendael complex — now a protected national monument — was once such a summer residence outside the city.

A country estate in Amsterdam

The estate we now know as Frankendael was probably founded not long after the draining of the Watergraafsmeer (1628-1629). The first plots were issued in 1631, but twenty years later the lake flooded and had to be drained again. After that second reclamation, Nicolaas van Liebergen founded a homestead in 1660 on the corner of the Middenweg and the Grote Tochtsloot: a symmetrically built house with a matching ornamental and kitchen garden. In 1672 the polder was deliberately flooded once more, on the orders of the city council, to hold off the approaching French army.

When Izaak Balde became the owner in 1695, the homestead consisted of “huysinge, bepotinge, beplantinge en verder getimmerte” — house, plantings and outbuildings. He named the estate Frankendael, after Frankenthal, the Protestant refuge of his grandfather in the Palatinate near Worms. He extended the grounds and around 1733 gave the house its current appearance: a tall corps de logis flanked by lower coach houses and stables, decorated in the Dutch Louis XV style with scrollwork, festoons and rocaille motifs. At the rear an extension was added with a fine view over the ornamental garden, once lavishly laid out with topiary, coloured shells and a great variety of flowers.

Original interior elements

While the garden changed a great deal, the eighteenth-century layout and finishes — stucco and panelling — largely survive inside. On the main floor all the original wooden folding shutters and their casings remain. The front door opens onto a hallway on the central axis of the house, onto which three double doors with arched mouldings open. Walls and ceiling are white stucco with ornaments in the Dutch Louis XIV style; this refinement dates from the time of construction (circa 1733), presumably commissioned by Izaak Balde. At the end of the hallway lies the protruding garden room. In the basement the joists are exposed, and a kitchen hearth and fly cupboard from Balde’s great rebuilding survive.

From estate to nursery

From the early nineteenth century Frankendael was permanently inhabited. In 1866 the house became the property of the Royal Dutch Horticultural Society Linnaeus, which used it as offices, dining hall and library. In 1882 the municipality housed the offices and horticultural school of the City Nursery here, and in 1894 it became the residence of the head of Municipal Plantings.

Traces of the last residents

In 1957 the municipality offered Frankendael as an official residence to city architect Ben Merkelbach. His descendants lived and worked in the building until 2004. The traces of all these successive users give the house an extra cultural-historical dimension: on the main floor, a wall mosaic designed by visual artist and illustrator Noni Lichtveld (daughter of the writer Albert Helman) recalls the final period of habitation. The mosaic sits in the simple modern kitchen of 1957, designed by Merkelbach himself, and depicts objects and figures from Keizersgracht 574, where the Merkelbach-Lichtveld family lived until 1957.

Sculptures and gate

The current layout of garden and park shows several phases of use. The rectangular house plot, moated and ringed by tree belts, probably took shape under Izaak Balde. In 1770 the marble sculpture group of the river gods Neptune, Amphitrite and Arion with its shell-shaped basin was placed at the front, signed “I(gnatius) van Logteren, 1714” and originating from the Driemond estate near Weesp. On the forecourt stand four sculptures from around 1700, probably placed by Jan Gildemeester, owner from 1759: Ceres with a sheaf of corn, Bacchus with a goblet of grapes, King David with crown and harp, and a boy’s head that presumably served as the base of a vase.

In 1783 the classicist wooden gate was erected to a design by Jacob Otten Husly (1738-1796), architect of Felix Meritis, commissioned by Jan Gildemeester Janszoon. His coat of arms — a bent, armoured arm holding a bone — appears on the back of the gate, as it does in the centre panel of the roof balustrade of the house itself.

Hermitage

Anthony Dull, the first owner to live at Frankendael permanently, added the brick hermitage in the early nineteenth century, set in a constructed landscape along a winding waterway. Such a hermitage was a beloved feature of landscape gardens — now called a “folly”, but meant sincerely at the time: a place to imagine yourself in nature and let your thoughts wander.

From tea garden to nursery

From 1835 the park was partly opened to “the civilised public” and Frankendael quickly grew into a much-loved pleasure and tea garden. The tea garden closed in 1866, when the Linnaeus society turned the park into a tree nursery; in 1882 the grounds became the City Nursery. During the Second World War architectural fragments were stored at Frankendael, including pieces of the Nieuwezijds Chapel and of the façade “Het Moortje” at Herengracht 126.

Garden and park today

Large parts of the four lime-tree belts around the house plot remain intact. From the late eighteenth century a landscape layout with winding paths and hillocks emerged in phases; part of it later became a botanical instruction garden. At the start of this century the Oost/Watergraafsmeer district returned Frankendael to public use: in 2003-2004 the house plot was restored and a quasi-eighteenth-century symmetrical garden with flower beds, low box hedges and parterres was laid out behind the house.

During excavation work in August 2003, masonry was discovered behind the coach house: the remains of an eighteenth-century greenhouse or orangery, demolished around 1820-1830. A photograph from 1873 shows the building as “Frankendael tea dome (former Orangery)”, and on an 1883 map it is recognisable as an aviary on the north side of the estate. After the archaeological research the remains disappeared under the turf once more.
